I study where atmospheric turbulence occurs, how it forms, and how we can better anticipate its impacts on aviation. My research connects high-resolution radiosonde and aircraft observations with numerical weather prediction and climate-model experiments, linking small-scale atmospheric processes to forecasting and a changing climate.

Observing and estimating atmospheric turbulence

I develop methods that turn high-resolution weather-balloon observations into estimates of atmospheric turbulence. Our minimum-Richardson-number approach accounts for wind shear and atmospheric stability, extending estimation beyond temperature overturns to include turbulence-prone, statically stable layers.

A sounding from Grand Junction, Colorado, illustrates what the method adds. On 19 April 2023, it identified all three overturning layers captured by the Thorpe method and additional layers with positive Richardson numbers below 0.25, associated with strong vertical wind shear. The dissipation estimates in the overturning layers were comparable in magnitude to the shear-based Thorpe formulation. This case shows how the approach extends turbulence estimation into statically stable, strongly sheared conditions.

From individual profiles to global patterns

Applying this method to ten years of observations (2015-2024) from 336 stations reveals how estimated turbulence varies with altitude, season, and location. Shear-associated turbulence is more frequent in winter, whereas overturning turbulence in the troposphere peaks in summer. This record helps distinguish turbulence regimes and provides a complementary resource for evaluating forecasts where aircraft measurements are sparse.

Understanding how turbulence forms

I combine observations with high-resolution numerical simulations to investigate the processes that generate turbulence. For a case over Pennsylvania on 7 February 2020, I used the Weather Research and Forecasting (WRF) model to connect the observed turbulence to its atmospheric environment.

The simulation reproduced the broad wind and geopotential patterns in ERA5 and key features of the radar-observed convective system. It also captured the negative potential-temperature gradient near 7.5 km seen in the radiosonde profile, reproducing the overturning signature used by the Thorpe method. Agreement across these scales provides a basis for examining the physical processes behind this event.

Improving aviation turbulence forecasts

In collaboration with ECMWF, I evaluated 12-hour forecasts from its Integrated Forecasting System clear-air turbulence product (IFS-CAT) against commercial-aircraft observations from October 2022 to September 2023. The forecast map below shows the predicted eddy dissipation rate at 200 hPa, while the accompanying receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves summarize performance over the full year.

Forecast skill was strongest at 6-15 km, and ensemble-mean forecasts had higher areas under the ROC curve than deterministic forecasts. However, at the moderate-or-greater turbulence threshold, detection probabilities were below 0.1 across all altitude ranges. This gap between overall discrimination and detection of stronger events motivated the recalibration.

Recalibration and forecast improvement

We developed two recalibration approaches: Option 1 updates the reference turbulence distribution using ten years of aircraft observations, while Option 2 optimizes the distribution parameters to improve skill while limiting false alarms. Both concentrate higher predicted intensities into more localized regions and reduce weaker background values.

Tests for January and July 2023 showed improvements in both ROC skill and moderate-or-greater turbulence detection across three altitude ranges, with larger gains from Option 2. Independent observations from the 2019 SouthTRAC research-aircraft campaign also supported the improved intensity distribution. This work connects observed turbulence intensity with model predictions to support more reliable aviation guidance.

Turbulence in a changing climate

Observed changes in fine-scale wind shear

How is the atmosphere changing at the small vertical scales relevant to turbulence? I address this question using high-resolution radiosonde observations. An analysis of 20 years of measurements from 68 stations across the contiguous United States revealed stronger increases in fine-scale vertical wind shear than in conventional bulk-layer estimates.

Between 300 and 200 hPa, the annual mean of profile-maximum shear increased by 7.4% per decade during 2005-2024. These observations highlight the importance of resolving thin shear layers when assessing atmospheric change and potential aviation hazards. The result describes a regional wind-shear trend; it does not directly measure an increase in turbulence encounters.

Climate change and solar geoengineering

I investigate how climate change and proposed climate interventions could reshape the atmospheric conditions associated with aviation turbulence. Using simulations from three climate models, we compared warming scenarios with stratospheric aerosol injection experiments.

By 2069, globally averaged annual counts of diagnosed moderate-or-greater turbulence at 200 hPa increased relative to 2024 under warming, but declined below the 2024 reference level in the aerosol-injection scenarios analyzed. The size and geographical pattern of these changes varied across models and scenarios. Because these climate models do not resolve aircraft-scale turbulence directly, the results concern diagnosed turbulence potential. This research examines how different climate pathways could affect aviation-relevant atmospheric conditions.
